Working Papers
Under Review Informed Choice Increases Perceived Quality Gap
with Balázs Krusper  ·  formerly: How Does Choice Affect Beliefs?
We study in the lab how choosing one of two products affects beliefs about their qualities. Using a between-subject design, we randomize both ownership mode (choice vs. assignment) and whether a product appears favorable by pairing it with an objectively better or worse alternative. We find that choosing magnifies the belief gap between the owned and unowned alternative, driven almost entirely by newly formed downwardly distorted beliefs about rejected options. Additionally, prompting participants to focus on product qualities before choosing attenuates this effect — suggesting attention is a key driver. The results provide support for active choice policies over opt-out defaults.
Working Paper Overconfidence Due to a Self-reliance Dilemma
with Nikola Frollová
Choosing between payment based on one's own or others' performance is inherent in most delegation decisions. We propose and test that such self-reliance dilemmas result in motivated reasoning. Learning about the dilemma decreases participants' beliefs about their counterpart's performance advantage by an average of 17%, causes overestimation of own performance, and increases the fraction who falsely believe they outperformed their counterpart. Organizations should carefully manage delegation decisions and implement measures to curb overconfidence.
Working Paper Choice-induced Sticky Learning
with Balázs Krusper
We study how choosing a product affects learning about products after the choice. Participants who choose investments respond less to price changes than those who receive investments exogenously — for both own and non-owned investments, and for both good and bad news. A structural model shows that learning is close to Bayesian after exogenous allocation, while it is too sticky after making a choice.
Working Paper Excusing Beliefs about Third-party Success
Single-authored
I investigate whether people distort beliefs about third parties — such as the ability of scientists to offset one's environmental impact — to excuse self-interested behavior. In a lab experiment, beliefs are 13 percentage points higher when it is the failure (rather than success) of a third party that results in taking money from passive participants. The results suggest that people use belief-based excuses about third-party success.

In Progress Sinners and Saints: How Narratives Affect Moral Behaviour
with Dan McGee
We model decision problems where agents care about their self-image and choose beliefs about others' moral actions in a flexible way. We establish a hypothesis testing equilibrium (HTE) in which agents hold self-serving beliefs about others' behavior, provided these beliefs are rationalizable and sufficiently plausible. We analyze how increased behavioral visibility in society affects overall morality — finding it is detrimental when saints are praised, but desirable when sinners are punished.

About

I am a behavioral economist specializing in how people form beliefs and make decisions in areas that are personally important to them — particularly in economic situations with real stakes.

My research uses both theoretical models and laboratory experiments to isolate the key cognitive and motivational forces that shape beliefs and choices. A recurring theme across my work is motivated reasoning: the tendency to arrive at beliefs that are convenient rather than accurate.

I have also collaborated with large international teams on questions of organizational inequality, working with administrative linked employer-employee datasets across many countries and applying methods from empirical microeconomics.

Currently, I am a postdoc at the Faculty of Economics and Administration at Masaryk University in Brno.
